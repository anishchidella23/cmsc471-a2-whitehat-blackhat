Two ways to tell the same story about women in higher education

Both charts below are built from one World Bank table: tertiary (university-level) enrollment rates for women and men, 192 countries, 1970–2018. Neither chart invents a number. The first tries to show what the data supports. The second uses the same file to argue something the data does not support, and the write-up underneath it explains exactly how.

White hat

Women overtook men in universities — in richer countries first, and not everywhere

Median female-to-male tertiary enrollment ratio by World Bank income group. The shaded band is the interquartile range: half the countries in that group fall inside it. A ratio of 1.0 means equal numbers of women and men enrolled relative to population.

Coverage is not constant. The number of countries reporting rises from 25 in 1970 to 118 in 2017, so earlier years describe a smaller and richer set of countries. Years where a group has fewer than five reporting countries are not drawn.

The same year, country by country

Every country reporting in 2017, grouped by income. Hover a dot for the underlying numbers.

Write-up: design choices

The dataset is the World Bank's gender indicator collection, from which we used two series: gross tertiary enrollment for women and for men, as a percentage of the population of university age. We joined each country to its World Bank region and income group, dropped the 48 aggregate entities (“World”, “Euro area” and similar) so that no country is counted twice, and dropped rows where either sex was missing. Venezuela is absent for a different reason: the World Bank leaves its income classification blank, and our grouping requires one. That leaves 4,651 country-years across 192 countries. The derived measure is the ratio of the two rates, which is what makes countries at very different enrollment levels comparable: Finland and Ethiopia can both be asked the same question, “for every man enrolled, how many women are?”

The headline finding is real and worth stating plainly: the median country crossed parity around 2000, and by 2017 the median ratio was 1.24. But presenting that single number would hide the more important pattern, which is that the crossover is stratified by income. In 2017 the median ratio was 1.36 in high-income countries and 1.27 in upper-middle-income ones, but 1.03 in lower-middle-income countries and 0.53 in low-income countries, where all seven reporting countries were below parity. A woman in a low-income country is still enrolled at about half the rate of a man there. Splitting by income group is therefore the main design decision: it is the split that changes the conclusion.

Three choices follow from wanting the reader to be able to check us. The y axis starts at zero and is linear, so the vertical distance between two ratios means what it appears to mean. The interquartile band is drawn because a median alone would imply more agreement within a group than exists. And the small grey chart below the main one reports how many countries are in the data each year, because the single most misleading thing about this dataset is that its early years cover only 25 mostly wealthy countries — any 1970-to-today comparison is not like-for-like, and the reader should be able to see that rather than take our word for it.

The dot plot answers the question the trend lines cannot: how much spread is inside each group. It shows that “high income” still contains three countries below parity, and that the low-income group is not merely lower but barely overlaps the others. One honest limitation remains visible in the data: gross enrollment ratios can exceed 100% because they count enrolled students of any age against the population of a standard age band, which is why 86 country-years show female rates above 100%. We kept the raw measure rather than capping it, and say so here.

Black hat

The Male Education Collapse

Men are falling further behind in higher education every year. The gap has nearly quadrupled since 1995, and the trend shows no sign of reversing.

Source: World Bank gender statistics, tertiary enrollment, 1995–2018.

Write-up: how this chart misleads

Every number in the black hat chart is computed from the same file as the white hat chart. It nonetheless argues something false — that men worldwide are being left behind in education — using techniques from all three categories.

Data transformation. The chart silently drops every low-income and lower-middle-income country, keeping only high-income and upper-middle-income ones. That removes 40 of the 118 countries reporting in 2017, and the removed group has a median ratio of 0.91 — precisely the countries where women are still behind. The caption says “World Bank gender statistics” without mentioning the filter, so the reader assumes global coverage. The start year of 1995 is also chosen rather than given: it is close to the crossover point, which makes the series look like it begins at rough equality and departs from it. Starting in 1970, where the same panel shows women far behind, would destroy the story. The companion chart counts countries where women lead cumulatively, so the line can only ever rise — a country that slips back below parity is never subtracted.

Marks and encodings. The y axis is a power scale with exponent 2.1, not a linear one. Equal vertical distances therefore represent increasingly small differences, which bends a curve that is actually flattening into one that appears to accelerate. The real means are +4.1 points in 1995, +9.8 in 2000, +15.2 in 2010 and +15.9 in 2018 — most of the movement happened before 2010 and the last eight years are nearly flat, which the chart's shape denies. The axis is also not zero-anchored, the filled gradient adds visual mass that implies accumulating harm, and the red is doing rhetorical work that the numbers do not.

Titles and labels. “The Male Education Collapse” describes a decline, but male enrollment has risen throughout the period in absolute terms; only the gap relative to women has grown. The y axis reads “male enrollment shortfall,” a framing with no technical meaning that presents a difference between two rates as a deficit owed to men. The subtitle's “nearly quadrupled” is arithmetically true of 4.1 to 15.9 and chosen because a ratio of two small differences sounds more alarming than the differences themselves. Nothing states that these are gross enrollment ratios, which can exceed 100% and are not headcounts.

The intended effect is a chart that looks careful — it has a source line, axis labels, annotated endpoints and a restrained palette — while the deception sits in the filter and the axis exponent, neither of which is visible without reading the code or the underlying data.

Data and references

  • Data source: World Bank Gender Statistics, series School enrollment, tertiary, female (% gross) and School enrollment, tertiary, male (% gross), via the World Bank Data by Indicators compilation linked from the assignment handout. Region and income group come from that repository's country metadata file.
  • Processed file used by both charts: data/tertiary_enrollment.csv — one row per country-year with both enrollment rates, their ratio, region and income group. Aggregates and rows missing either sex were removed.
  • Known bias in the source: country coverage grows from 25 countries in 1970 to 118 in 2017, and poorer countries report less consistently throughout.
  • Correll, M. and Heer, J. Black Hat Visualization (2017) — framing for the black hat techniques.
  • Cairo, A., How Visualizations Lie; Parikh, R., How to Lie with Data Visualization.

Development process

Team: Anish Chidella and Varun Kota.

How the work was divided: we scoped the project together — choosing the dataset, agreeing on what the honest finding was, and deciding what the black hat chart would try to make a reader believe. From there Anish took the data pipeline and the white hat view (reshaping the World Bank file, joining income groups, the trend chart with its interquartile bands and the coverage disclosure) and Varun took the black hat view and its deception strategy (the income filter, the power-scale axis, the cumulative counter and the framing of the titles). We each drafted the write-up for the chart we built and then edited the other's, which was deliberate: the person who did not build a chart is a better test of whether its write-up actually explains it. Deployment and the demo video were handled jointly.

Process: the work went in four stages — finding a dataset whose honest story and misleading story could both be told from one file; reshaping it (joining income groups, dropping aggregates, deriving the ratio); building the two visualizations; and writing up the techniques. The longest stage by far was the first: most candidate framings either had no interesting deception available or required a lie rather than a distortion. Deciding that the income-group split was the honest finding is what made the black hat chart possible, because the deception could then be omission of that split rather than a fabricated number.

Time: roughly 24 people-hours in total, split close to evenly between us. The rough breakdown: 7 hours settling on the dataset and the framing, 3 on reshaping the data and checking what it would and would not support, 5 on the white hat view, 5 on the black hat view, 3 on the write-ups, and about 1 on deployment.

Two stages ran long for different reasons. Choosing the framing took the most calendar time because most candidate stories offered no interesting deception — the misleading version would have had to be a lie rather than a distortion, which is a weaker answer to this assignment. Tuning the black hat took the most iterations: the first version leaned on a truncated axis, which reads as a trick immediately, and several passes were needed before the distortion moved somewhere a reader cannot see without opening the data.

AI usage

We used an AI coding agent (Claude Code, Anthropic) throughout, and it did write most of the D3. That part is ordinary. What is worth documenting is the three places where it changed a design decision rather than typing one out.

1. It found the story before any chart existed. Our first instinct was the obvious framing: women have overtaken men in higher education, with the black hat chart denying it. Instead of drawing that, we had the agent profile the dataset first. It reported that the global median ratio crosses parity around 2000 but that the crossover is stratified — 1.36 in high-income countries against 0.53 in low-income ones — and, separately, that the number of reporting countries grows from 25 to 118 over the period. Those two facts restructured the whole assignment. The white hat chart became a split by income group because that is the split that changes the conclusion, and the black hat chart became an argument built on omitting that split. The deception is an omission of real structure rather than a fabricated number, which is a strictly better black hat, and we would not have found it by starting from the chart.

2. It was used adversarially, to test subtlety. The rubric rewards deception that survives close reading, which is hard to judge when you already know where the trick is. We used the agent as a stand-in critic: given only the rendered black hat chart and its caption, which claims can be checked and which require the underlying file? That exchange is what moved the deception out of the visible layer. An earlier draft leaned on a truncated axis, which any careful reader catches from the tick labels; the final version moves the weight onto the income filter and a power scale with exponent 2.1, neither of which leaves a visible trace in the chart.

3. It caught a problem that became a white hat feature. Reviewing the rendered page, the agent flagged that Qatar's ratio of 7.7 was compressing every other country into the left third of the dot plot. The quick fix is to drop the outlier — which would have been a quiet black hat move in the middle of our white hat chart. Instead we capped the axis at 2.5, drew the two countries beyond it as outlined dots at the cap, and named them with their underlying numbers in the caption. The constraint produced a better transparency decision than we had planned.

Verification. Because an AI drafted the prose, every quantitative claim on this page was recomputed from the processed CSV rather than taken from the draft: the 2017 medians by income group (0.53 / 1.03 / 1.27 / 1.36), the coverage range (25 to 118 countries), the black hat panel's mean gaps (+4.1, +9.8, +15.2, +15.9 points), and the 40 countries its filter removes. Treating the agent's prose as a claim to be checked rather than as output to be pasted is the habit we would carry into any future project.
